# Physics Engine Review: `environment_physics.cpp` & `simulation.cpp`

I've conducted a deep-dive review of the physics models, equations, and integration logic in the provided C++ files. 

The implementation of the Forrestal cavity expansion (N-factor for ogive noses), the Tate-Alekseevskii hypervelocity erosion, the CEB-FIP dynamic increase factors, and the 1976 Standard Atmosphere are **exceptionally accurate**. 

However, I found **3 critical physics flaws** in how these models interact, which will cause the simulation to produce physically impossible results (instant melting, infinite bending, and reverse gravity).

---

### 1. The "Reverse Gravity" Coordinate Mismatch
**Location:** `angleSimulation` & `simulateAtmosphericDrop`

**The Physics Problem:** 
In `simulateAtmosphericDrop`, gravity is *added* to the Y-velocity (`d.dv_y = cons.gravity - ...`), which dictates that **Positive Y = Downwards**. 
However, in `angleSimulation`, the initial velocity is set via:
```cpp
double current_vy = velocity * std::sin(fpa_rad);
```
In aerospace, a descending Flight Path Angle (FPA) is negative (e.g., -90° for a vertical drop). Because `sin(-90°) = -1`, `current_vy` becomes negative. In your integration loop, a negative Y-velocity means the bomb is moving UP. Gravity will pull it down eventually, but the bomb will initially "fall upwards" in the simulation.

**The Fix:**
Reverse the sign of the trigonometric projection if FPA is assumed to be standard (negative for descent).
```cpp
double current_vy = -velocity * std::sin(fpa_rad);
```

### 2. Runaway Thermal Heating during Hydrodynamic Erosion
**Location:** `simulateGroundPenetration`

**The Physics Problem:**
During the Tate erosion phase, you calculate the heat generated by the target material flowing past the interface:
```cpp
double erosion_heat_rate = 0.5 * baseDensity * (v - u) * (v - u) * area * std::fabs(v - u);
d.dT = erosion_heat_rate / (safeMass * proj.specific_heat);
```
You are taking **100%** of the kinetic energy flux of the target material and dumping it into the *remaining* mass of the projectile. 

In reality, hypervelocity erosion is an adiabatic phase change at the interface. The vast majority of this energy (95-99%) is carried away by the ejected target material and the vaporized/eroded projectile mass (the ejecta). By dumping 100% of this energy into the remaining solid rod, the projectile will reach its melting point almost instantly, triggering a false "Thermal Destruction" failure.

**The Fix:**
Apply a heat partition fraction (Stanton number equivalent). Only a tiny fraction of the hydrodynamic friction conducts into the surviving rod.
```cpp
double heat_partition_fraction = 0.05; // 5% of energy conducts into the rod
d.dT = (erosion_heat_rate * heat_partition_fraction) / (safeMass * proj.specific_heat);
```

### 3. Infinite Asymmetric Bending (The J-Hook Flaw)
**Location:** `simulateGroundPenetration`

**The Physics Problem:**
You calculate the asymmetric lateral force causing bending moments as:
```cpp
asymmetric_force = (0.5 * baseDensity * squaredVelocity * area) * std::sin(obliquity_radians + angleOfAttack_radians);
```
You apply this continuously at all depths. 
While Angle of Attack (AOA) always causes lift/lateral force, **surface obliquity** only causes asymmetric pressure while the nose is *entering* the target (the cratering phase). Once the projectile is fully buried (Depth > 2×Diameter), it is surrounded by rock on all sides. The pressure equalizes, and surface obliquity no longer causes lateral force.

As currently written, if a bomb strikes at a 30° obliquity, it will experience massive lateral force forever, even 50 meters underground, guaranteeing a false structural failure (J-Hooking).

**The Fix:**
Fade out the obliquity contribution as the bomb buries itself, while keeping the AOA contribution active.
```cpp
double depth_ratio = std::clamp(current_depth / (2.0 * proj.diameter), 0.0, 1.0);
double active_obliquity = obliquity_radians * (1.0 - depth_ratio); // Fades to 0 when buried

asymmetric_force = (0.5 * baseDensity * squaredVelocity * area) * std::sin(active_obliquity + angleOfAttack_radians);
```

### Minor Note on Shock Impedance
In `shockWaveIgnition`, you use `Up = current_velocity / (1.0 + std::sqrt(rho_t / rho_p));`. 
Using the square root of the density ratio is a common acoustic approximation assuming bulk sound speeds ($c$) are identical ($Z = \rho c$). For a highly rigorous simulation, the 1D Rankine-Hugoniot match requires matching the $P-U_p$ curves using the shock velocities ($U_s = c_0 + s U_p$). However, for the purpose of a simulator, your current density-ratio approximation is entirely acceptable and computationally efficient. No immediate change needed unless you want strict hydrodynamic exactness.
